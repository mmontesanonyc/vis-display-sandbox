About estimates and confidence intervals

Surveys ask a sample of people in order to learn about everyone. The result is an estimate of the true value in the whole population, not the exact value.

For example, if we survey 100 people and 20 say they have a cat, that doesn’t mean exactly 20% of the whole city has a cat. A different group of 100 people might have given us 17% or 24%. So alongside the estimate (20%), we calculate a confidence interval: a range that, with a stated level of confidence (usually 95%), is expected to include the true value. How wide the interval is depends on how many people we surveyed, how much their answers vary, and how the survey was designed and weighted. In this example, the interval might run from about 12% to 28%.

Smaller groups mean wider intervals

Suppose we survey 10,000 New Yorkers:

  • For the whole sample, the interval is narrow, so we can pin down the estimate quite closely.
  • When we split answers by sex, each group has about 5,000 people. The intervals get somewhat wider.
  • When we split answers by neighborhood (there are 42 UHF neighborhoods), each has only a few hundred people on average. The intervals get much wider, so any one neighborhood’s estimate is much less precise.

Intervals help us compare groups

Say 20% of residents in Neighborhood A report having a dog, compared with 22% in Neighborhood B. It’s tempting to say B has higher dog ownership. But if A’s interval is 16% to 24% and B’s is 18% to 26%, the two overlap heavily. The 2-point gap could easily be due to chance in who we happened to survey, so we can’t conclude there’s a real difference.

In short: an estimate is our best single guess at the true value, based on asking a sample. The confidence interval shows how much uncertainty surrounds it. The narrower the interval, the more precise the estimate.
